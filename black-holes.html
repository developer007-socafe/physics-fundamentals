Black Holes & Gravitation

Where gravity wins, and what it leaves behind

1. The Event Horizon

A black hole is not a hole in space. It is a region of spacetime where the curvature is so extreme that every possible future-directed path leads to the same place: the singularity. The boundary of that region is the event horizon, and it is not a physical surface — nothing special happens as you cross it. Locally, an infalling observer notices nothing unusual.

For a non-rotating, uncharged black hole — a Schwarzschild black hole — the horizon sits at the Schwarzschild radius, the single length scale you can build from a mass and the speed of light:

$$r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}$$

Note how little else enters. The radius of a black hole depends on its mass and nothing else — no material, no chemical composition. A teaspoon of neutron-star matter crushed to $3.2 \times 10^{30}$ kg would have $r_s \approx 4.7$ km, smaller than a city.

Radial structure of a Schwarzschild black hole. Outside the dashed horizon light can still escape; inside, every future path ends at the singularity.
Escape velocity intuition — in Newtonian gravity, escape velocity is $v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{2GM/r}$. Set $v_\text{esc} = c$ and you recover $r_s = 2GM/c^2$. The Newtonian argument is a mnemonic here, not a derivation, but the answer it gives is exactly right.
$$v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} = c \;\Longrightarrow\; r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}$$
Example. The Sun, were it replaced by a black hole of the same mass, would have $r_s \approx 3.0$ km — but it would not be brighter in the sky. Black holes have no surface to emit from. The danger is purely gravitational: the orbit of Earth would be badly disturbed at a distance of about $10^9$ m, roughly a thousand times the original orbit.

2. Gravitational Redshift

Light climbing out of a gravitational well loses energy. To a distant observer the light is redshifted; to one hovering just above the horizon it is blueshifted. For a static observer at radius $r$ outside a mass $M$:

$$\frac{\nu_\infty}{\nu_r} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - r_s/r}}$$

The clock rate goes with it. Proper time for the hovering observer, relative to a clock at infinity:

$$d\tau = \sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{r}}\; dt$$

As $r \to r_s$ the factor vanishes — to an external observer a clock at the horizon appears to freeze forever. This is the origin of the "frozen star" pictures you may have seen. It is a coordinate illusion: the infalling observer crosses the horizon in finite proper time.

$$t_\text{cross} = \int_{r_s}^{r} \frac{dr'}{\sqrt{2GM/r' - 2GM/r}} = \text{finite}$$
Where this is measurable — gravitational redshift is confirmed by the Pound–Rebka experiment (1960), which shifted light by a factor of $2.5 \times 10^{-15}$ across Earth's height, and by the Thermal Atmosphere Measurement package on GPS satellites.

3. Tidal Forces & Spaghettification

"Infinitely dense" is wrong and dangerously so. The singularity is not a place — it is a place time runs out. What actually destroys an infalling object is the differential gravity across it: your feet feel a stronger pull than your head, and the object is stretched.

The stretching is quantified by the geodesic deviation equation, whose Riemann curvature term sets the scale:

$$\frac{D^2 \xi^\mu}{D\tau^2} = -R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}\, u^\nu \xi^\alpha u^\beta$$

For a radial infall outside a Schwarzschild hole, the leading tidal term scales as $1/r^3$, with an explicit Newtonian form:

$$\Delta a \approx \frac{2GM}{r^3}\,\Delta x$$

The mass factorises out and the distance appears cubed. That is why a supermassive black hole is a gentle place. At the event horizon of $M_\odot = 4.6 \times 10^{30}$ kg the tidal stretch is survivable across a human body; for $10 M_\odot$ it is not; near a stellar-mass hole, $3 \times 10^{29}$ kg, the tidal force is spread over $r_s \approx 400$ m and shreds you.

Differential gravity stretches a body along the radial direction. Because the tidal term scales as $1/r^3$, the closer the approach, the more violent — and the larger $M$, the further out this begins.
Spaghettification — the tidal stretching that shreds an infalling object. The observed "falling into a black hole" footage from Interstellar's fictional Gargantua was based on scientific papers on tidal disruption, though the effects there were exaggerated.

4. Accretion Disks & Jets

A black hole has no surface, so matter crossing the horizon does not radiate on the way in. Instead, angular momentum slows the infalling gas into a disk — friction between neighbouring rings converts kinetic energy into heat. The inner edge of the disk sits at the innermost stable circular orbit, $r_\text{ISCO} = 3r_s$ for a Schwarzschild hole.

$$r_\text{ISCO} = \frac{6GM}{c^2} = 3\,r_s$$

Efficiency is the remarkable part. Radiating at the Eddington limit, a thin disk around a non-rotating black hole converts only about $5.7\%$ of the accreted rest mass into energy; spinning one up near-extremally raises that to roughly $42\%$. For ordinary fusion the figure is $0.7\%$.

$$\eta = \frac{L}{\dot{M}c^2} \approx 0.057 \;\to\; 0.42 \quad \text{(spin)}\qquad \text{vs} \quad 0.007 \;\text{for H}\!\to\!\text{He fusion}$$
Edge-on view. The disk extends far beyond the horizon, but its radiating inner edge is at $3r_s$ — that is where the light comes from.
Tidal disruption events — when a star wanders too close to a stellar-mass black hole, it is torn apart. Half the debris returns on highly eccentric orbits, and the resulting accretion burst is a tidal disruption flare, bright enough to be visible from other galaxies.

5. Hawking Radiation

Quantum mechanics makes black holes glow. The uncertainty principle forbids the vacuum from having sharply defined energy everywhere; near the horizon, a virtual particle–antiparticle pair straddling the membrane can escape while its partner falls in. Energy conservation is satisfied by the hole's mass shrinking.

$$T_\text{H} = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi G M k_B}$$

The inverse mass dependence is the whole story. For the Sun ($T_\text{H} \approx 6 \times 10^{-8}$ K) this is utterly negligible, and the hole would need $10^{10}$ years to evaporate. But for a microscopic primordial black hole of about $10^{11}$ kg, the temperature reaches $10^{11}$ K and it would have evaporated within the age of the universe.

$$M(t) \approx M_0\left(1 - \frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad \tau \approx 5120\pi G^2 M_0^3/(\hbar c^4) \approx 8.4\times10^{-17}\left(\frac{M_0}{\mathrm{kg}}\right)^3 \mathrm{s}$$

Mass scales as the cube of lifetime. This is why primordial black holes, if they exist, are a candidate for dark matter — a population in a narrow mass window would still be cooling, and would be genuinely "warm" rather than starlight.

Unresolved. Hawking radiation has never been observed. Every laboratory attempt has been swamped by backgrounds many orders of magnitude larger. It remains one of the few predictions of general relativity that general quantum theory has never rendered unstable — the "trans-Planckian problem" is exactly that it assumes validity at energies where nothing justifies it.

6. Gravitational Lensing

Mass curves spacetime, so light does not travel in straight lines. A compact mass acts as a lens. The deflection for a ray passing impact parameter $b$:

$$\alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2 b} = \frac{2r_s}{b}$$

The thin-lens approximation relates the observed angular separation $\theta$ to the Einstein radius $\theta_E$, from which lensing becomes a direct mass probe independent of any emission from the lens itself:

$$\beta = \theta - \frac{\theta_E^2}{\theta}, \qquad \theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\frac{D_{LS}}{D_L D_S}}$$
Exact alignment of source, lens and observer gives an Einstein ring — a perfect circle, because the deflection is the same at every angle around the lens.

7. The Observational Evidence

For decades black holes were theoretical. Three observations settled the question, all of them based on the same principle: you measure the mass by watching what orbits it.

Sagittarius A* — the radio source at the centre of our Galaxy. Stellar orbits measured by the Keck and VLT telescopes over more than a decade give a mass of about $4.3 \times 10^6\,M_\odot$ concentrated within a region smaller than our Solar System. No other object of that mass fits the data.
M87* — imaged in 2019 by the Event Horizon Telescope. A ring of emission $5.4 \times 10^{10} M_\odot$ in mass and $2.6 \times 10^{12}$ m across. The shadow's angular size confirms general relativity's prediction to within a few percent.
GW150914 — detected by LIGO in 2015, the first direct observation of gravitational waves: two $\sim 36\,M_\odot$ black holes spiralling together and merging into a $\sim 62\,M_\odot$ remnant, radiating about $3\,M_\odot c^2$ in energy. Since 2015 the catalog has passed 90 candidates.
Why the shadow is the right size — for a Schwarzschild hole the shadow subtends an angle of $2\sqrt{3}\,r_s/D$. The critical impact parameter for photon capture is $b_c = 3\sqrt{3}\,GM/c^2 = 2.6\,r_s$, which is why lensed images do not vanish smoothly at the shadow edge but jump.

Key Takeaways

  • A black hole is a region, not an object: everything inside $r_s = 2GM/c^2$ reaches the singularity. Nothing marks the horizon itself.
  • Gravity redshift makes clocks at $r$ run slow by $\sqrt{1 - r_s/r}$ — a coordinate effect that the infaller does not experience.
  • Tidal forces, not density, do the killing, and they scale as $1/r^3$. Bigger black holes are gentler.
  • Accretion is efficient because friction in the disk radiates — up to 42% with spin, versus 0.7% for fusion.
  • Hawking temperature scales as $1/M$, so only microscopic holes would evaporate within the age of the universe.
  • We now have strong evidence: Sgr A*, the M87* image, and dozens of gravitational-wave mergers.
